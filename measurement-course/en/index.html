Item Analysis: From Data to Decision

After the results were announced, a parent objected: “My son chose the third option in Question 7, and I am certain it is the correct one.” This week we learn how to detect an error like this before it happens, and how to decide the fate of every item in a test on evidence rather than impressions.

One hour of concepts and two hours of applied workshop · Course outcomes 3 and 5 · Prepares for Phase 3 of the project

By the end of the week you will be able to

  • Calculate difficulty, discrimination and the corrected item-total correlation, and verify them by hand
  • Interpret a distractor analysis and distinguish an effective distractor from a flawed one
  • Estimate the effect of deleting or correcting an item on test reliability
  • Make a justified decision for each item that weighs the numbers against the importance of the objective
  • Use Claude for the calculations, and know what you must verify yourself

Difficulty index

The proportion of students who answered correctly. The higher the value, the easier the item.

p = correct / N

The desirable range depends on the purpose: for ranking students, roughly between 0.30 and 0.80; for a mastery test of essential objectives, 0.85 or higher may be acceptable.

Upper–lower group discrimination

We rank students by total score and subtract the proportion answering correctly in the bottom 27% from the proportion in the top 27%.

D = p(upper) − p(lower)

Watch out for students tied at the group boundary: in the workshop data the upper group has 45 students, not 41.

Corrected item-total correlation

The correlation between the item and the rest of the score after the item is removed from it. It is more precise than the upper–lower index because it uses all students, and it is not inflated because the item does not “measure itself”.

Below 0.20 is weak, 0.30 or above is good, and a negative value calls for checking the answer key first.

Distractors

A good distractor attracts those who do not know and does not attract those who do. A distractor chosen by fewer than 5% is not working, and one chosen more by the upper group than the lower group needs review.

Reliability and the standard error

Reliability measures the consistency of scores, and the standard error of measurement translates it into score points: in the workshop data it equals 2.31, so a two-point difference is not enough to conclude that one student is better than another.

Statistics guide, they do not decide

Four questions, in order: Is the key correct? Is the wording clear? Is the level appropriate? How important is the objective the item measures? No software can answer the last question.

The workshop in 60 minutes

  1. Explore the Excel file without Claude, and guess why one item is flawed (10 min)
  2. Upload the file to Claude and paste the ready-made prompt (15 min)
  3. Verify: match at least three values against the Excel file before any interpretation (10 min)
  4. Diagnose the flawed items, and fill in the decision table yourself (15 min)
  5. The decisive experiment: correct the key of Item 7 and compare reliability before and after (10 min)
  6. For fast groups: ask for a replacement distractor for Item 23 and evaluate it

Ready-made prompt for Step 2

You have an Excel file for a 30-item multiple-choice test administered to 150 students. The "Responses" sheet contains the raw responses, and its second row contains the answer key. Carry out the analysis by running actual code on the file, and show the code you used. For each item, calculate: 1. The difficulty index. 2. The discrimination index using upper and lower groups of 27%, stating explicitly how you handled students tied in score at the boundary of each group, and how many students are in each group. 3. The corrected correlation between the item and the rest of the total score. 4. The Kuder–Richardson 20 coefficient for the test, and its value if each item is deleted. 5. The proportion choosing each option overall, and in the upper and lower groups. Then give me a single table of results rounded to two decimal places. Do not interpret the results yet.

Do not upload any file containing real students' names or ID numbers. Replace them with codes before uploading.
